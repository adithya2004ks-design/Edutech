Q11(a): The Earth’s atmosphere is divided into five distinct layers based on altitude and temperature variations, each performing specific functions essential for life and environmental stability. The troposphere is the lowest layer and extends up to about 12 kilometers above the Earth’s surface. It contains most of the atmospheric air, water vapour, and clouds, and all weather phenomena such as rainfall, storms, and winds occur in this layer. Above the troposphere lies the stratosphere, which extends up to around 50 kilometers. This layer contains the ozone layer, which absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the sun and protects living organisms on Earth. The mesosphere is the third layer and is the coldest region of the atmosphere. In this layer, meteoroids entering the Earth’s atmosphere burn up due to friction, preventing them from reaching the surface. The thermosphere lies above the mesosphere and experiences very high temperatures due to absorption of solar radiation. It plays an important role in radio communication because of the presence of ionized particles. The outermost layer is the exosphere, which gradually merges with outer space. Satellites orbit the Earth in this layer. Together, these atmospheric layers regulate temperature, support life, protect the planet, and enable communication systems.

Q12: Igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks are the three major categories of rocks, differentiated based on their origin and formation processes. Igneous rocks are formed from the cooling and solidification of molten magma or lava. When magma cools slowly beneath the Earth’s surface, it forms intrusive igneous rocks such as granite, which have large crystals. When lava cools rapidly on the Earth’s surface, extrusive igneous rocks like basalt are formed. Igneous rocks are generally hard, crystalline in nature, and do not contain fossils. Sedimentary rocks are formed through the deposition, compaction, and cementation of sediments derived from the weathering and erosion of pre-existing rocks, as well as from organic materials. These sediments accumulate in layers over long periods, forming rocks such as sandstone, limestone, and shale. Sedimentary rocks are usually layered or stratified and often contain fossils, making them important for studying the history of life and past environmental conditions on Earth. Metamorphic rocks are formed when existing igneous or sedimentary rocks undergo transformation due to intense heat, pressure, or chemical processes within the Earth’s crust. This process, known as metamorphism, changes the mineral composition and structure of the original rock without melting it. Common examples of metamorphic rocks include marble, which is formed from limestone, and slate, which is formed from shale. Metamorphic rocks are typically harder and more compact than their parent rocks. These three types of rocks are interconnected through the rock cycle, a continuous process in which rocks change from one type to another over geological time. Together, igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks play a crucial role in shaping the Earth’s crust and providing valuable natural resources.

Q13(a): Vulnerability refers to the conditions that increase the susceptibility of individuals, communities, or systems to the impacts of disasters. One major type is physical vulnerability, which relates to the weakness of buildings, infrastructure, and critical facilities that may be damaged during disasters. Social vulnerability arises from factors such as age, gender, education, health status, and social inequality, which affect people’s ability to respond to and recover from disasters. Economic vulnerability is associated with poverty, unemployment, and lack of financial resources, limiting the capacity to prepare for disasters and recover from losses. Environmental vulnerability refers to the degradation of natural ecosystems, such as deforestation and loss of wetlands, which increases disaster risk. Assessing vulnerability involves both qualitative and quantitative approaches. Qualitative methods include community surveys, interviews, and participatory assessments that capture local knowledge and perceptions of risk. Quantitative approaches involve the use of indicators, indices, and statistical data to measure levels of vulnerability across regions or populations. Tools such as Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and remote sensing are widely used to map vulnerable areas and analyze exposure to hazards. Structural assessments are also conducted to evaluate the strength of buildings and infrastructure. By combining these approaches, vulnerability assessment provides a comprehensive understanding of risk and supports effective disaster mitigation and preparedness planning.

Q13(b): The two major physical vulnerability assessment approaches are structural analysis and exposure-based assessment. Structural analysis evaluates the strength, design, and materials of buildings and infrastructure. Exposure-based assessment examines the location of assets and populations in hazard-prone areas to estimate potential damage.

Q14(a): Risk assessment is a systematic process used to understand and evaluate the potential impacts of disasters. One of the key components is hazard identification, which involves identifying and analyzing potential hazards such as floods, earthquakes, cyclones, or industrial accidents that may affect a region. This step focuses on understanding the nature, frequency, and intensity of hazards. Another important component is vulnerability analysis, which examines the physical, social, economic, and environmental factors that increase the susceptibility of people, infrastructure, and systems to damage during disasters. Exposure assessment is also a crucial component of risk assessment. It involves identifying people, property, infrastructure, and economic activities that are located in hazard-prone areas and are likely to be affected during a disaster. Risk assessment further includes impact analysis, which estimates the potential consequences of hazards in terms of loss of life, property damage, environmental degradation, and economic disruption. Finally, risk evaluation is carried out to compare estimated risks against acceptable risk levels and prioritize areas requiring intervention. This step helps decision-makers determine appropriate mitigation and preparedness measures. Together, these components provide a comprehensive understanding of disaster risk and support effective planning, mitigation, and disaster risk management strategies.

Q14(b): Contemporary approaches to risk assessment use advanced tools such as Geographic Information Systems, remote sensing, probabilistic models, and computer simulations. These approaches enable accurate hazard mapping, real-time data analysis, scenario modeling, and better prediction of disaster risks for informed decision-making.

Q15(a): Disaster risk management consists of a set of core elements aimed at reducing the impacts of disasters and enhancing resilience. Prevention is the first element and focuses on avoiding disasters by restricting development in hazard-prone areas and enforcing safety regulations. Mitigation involves measures taken to reduce the severity of disasters, such as constructing embankments, cyclone shelters, and earthquake-resistant buildings. Preparedness is another essential element and includes planning, training, early warning systems, mock drills, and resource mobilization to ensure readiness before disasters occur. Response refers to immediate actions taken during and after a disaster, including rescue operations, relief distribution, emergency medical care, and temporary shelter provision. Recovery is the final element and involves restoring livelihoods, rebuilding infrastructure, and rehabilitating affected communities. It also emphasizes learning from past disasters to reduce future risks. These elements are interrelated and work together in a continuous cycle to minimize losses, protect lives and property, and promote sustainable development.

Q15(b): International relief organizations play a crucial role in disaster response and recovery by providing timely assistance and coordinated support to affected regions. During the response phase, these organizations supply emergency relief such as food, safe drinking water, temporary shelter, medical aid, and sanitation facilities to meet the immediate needs of disaster victims. They also support search and rescue operations, deploy medical teams, and help manage refugee camps in large-scale emergencies. Organizations such as the International Red Cross, United Nations agencies, and international non-governmental organizations work closely with national governments to ensure effective coordination and avoid duplication of efforts. \n\nIn the recovery phase, international relief organizations contribute to rehabilitation and reconstruction activities. They assist in rebuilding homes, schools, hospitals, and infrastructure, and support livelihood restoration through financial aid, skill development, and employment programs. These organizations also help strengthen local capacities by providing training, technical expertise, and resources for disaster preparedness and risk reduction. Through their global experience, funding support, and humanitarian principles, international relief organizations play an essential role in reducing suffering and promoting long-term resilience in disaster-affected communities.

Q16(a): Disaster response refers to the immediate and short-term actions taken to manage the effects of a disaster and reduce suffering. One major type is emergency response, which focuses on search and rescue operations, evacuation of affected populations, and provision of first aid to save lives. Relief response follows emergency actions and includes supplying essential needs such as food, safe drinking water, temporary shelter, clothing, and medical assistance to disaster victims. Another important type is medical and health response, which involves preventing disease outbreaks, providing trauma care, and ensuring mental health support through counselling services. Rehabilitation response is aimed at restoring basic services such as electricity, water supply, transportation, and communication systems. Reconstruction response is a longer-term effort that focuses on rebuilding damaged infrastructure, homes, and public facilities in a safer and more resilient manner. Additionally, coordination and logistics response ensures effective communication and cooperation among government agencies, relief organizations, and volunteers. Together, these types of disaster responses help stabilize affected communities, minimize losses, and support recovery after disasters.

Q16(b): Disaster mitigation measures are actions taken to reduce the severity and impact of disasters before they occur. These measures are broadly classified into structural and non-structural mitigation. Structural mitigation measures involve physical constructions designed to withstand or reduce disaster impacts. Examples include building dams and embankments to control floods, constructing cyclone shelters in coastal areas, and designing earthquake-resistant buildings using proper engineering standards. These measures help minimize damage to life and property during disasters. Non-structural mitigation measures focus on policies, planning, and awareness rather than physical structures. They include land-use planning to prevent settlements in hazard-prone areas, enforcement of building codes, disaster insurance, early warning systems, and public education programs. Non-structural measures also involve community training, preparedness planning, and environmental protection such as afforestation. Together, structural and non-structural mitigation measures play a vital role in reducing vulnerability, enhancing resilience, and ensuring sustainable development by minimizing the long-term impacts of disasters.

Q17(a): Participatory stakeholder engagement in disaster management refers to the active involvement of all relevant stakeholders in planning, decision-making, implementation, and evaluation of disaster risk reduction activities. Stakeholders include local communities, government agencies, non-governmental organizations, private sector entities, academic institutions, and volunteers. This approach recognizes that communities possess valuable local knowledge about hazards, vulnerabilities, and resources, which can significantly improve disaster preparedness and response. Engaging stakeholders ensures shared responsibility and promotes transparency, trust, and coordination among different actors. Community participation helps identify local risks, design appropriate mitigation measures, and develop realistic emergency plans. For example, involving residents in flood-prone areas in hazard mapping and evacuation planning improves early warning effectiveness and reduces casualties. Similarly, collaboration with local self-government bodies and NGOs in conducting mock drills and awareness programs enhances preparedness. Participatory engagement is also important during response and recovery phases. Community groups and volunteers often assist in rescue operations, relief distribution, and damage assessment. In recovery, stakeholder participation supports the concept of “Build Back Better” by ensuring that reconstruction meets local needs and improves resilience. Overall, participatory stakeholder engagement strengthens disaster management by combining technical expertise with local experience, leading to sustainable and inclusive disaster risk reduction strategies.

Q18(a): Effective disaster communication is essential for minimizing panic, ensuring public safety, and improving coordination during emergencies. The first step is message formulation, where accurate, clear, and consistent information about the disaster, risks, and protective actions is prepared by responsible authorities. This message must be simple and understandable to all sections of society. The second step is coordination among agencies, ensuring that government departments, emergency responders, and relief organizations communicate with a unified voice to avoid confusion. The third step involves selecting appropriate communication channels such as television, radio, mobile alerts, social media, and community networks to ensure timely dissemination of information. Accessibility and inclusiveness are important so that vulnerable groups, including elderly and disabled persons, receive the messages. The fourth step is timely dissemination, as delays in communication can increase casualties and damage. Feedback and monitoring form the next step, where authorities assess public response, correct misinformation, and update messages as the situation evolves. Finally, trust building is a crucial step in effective disaster communication. Transparent communication, credibility of information sources, and regular updates help build public trust and compliance with safety instructions. Together, these steps enhance disaster preparedness, response efficiency, and community resilience.

Q18(b): Risk communication focuses on informing and educating people about potential hazards and safety measures before a disaster occurs to improve preparedness. Crisis communication addresses immediate information needs during and after a disaster, guiding response actions, reducing panic, and supporting emergency management efforts.

Q20: The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction is a global agreement adopted in 2015 with the aim of substantially reducing disaster risks and losses in lives, livelihoods, health, and economic, physical, social, cultural, and environmental assets. The framework is guided by several key principles. It emphasizes that states have the primary responsibility for disaster risk reduction, while shared responsibility should be encouraged among governments, local authorities, the private sector, communities, and other stakeholders. It also promotes an all-of-society and all-of-state approach, highlighting the importance of inclusive participation, transparency, and accountability. Another guiding principle is the integration of disaster risk reduction into sustainable development policies and planning. The Sendai Framework sets seven global targets to be achieved by 2030. These include reducing global disaster mortality, decreasing the number of affected people, lowering direct economic losses, and reducing damage to critical infrastructure and disruption of basic services. It also aims to increase the number of countries with national and local disaster risk reduction strategies, enhance international cooperation with developing countries, and improve access to multi-hazard early warning systems and disaster risk information. The framework outlines four priorities for action. The first priority is understanding disaster risk by improving data collection, risk assessment, and knowledge sharing. The second priority focuses on strengthening disaster risk governance through effective institutions, laws, and coordination mechanisms. The third priority emphasizes investing in disaster risk reduction for resilience, including safer infrastructure and risk-informed development. The fourth priority is enhancing disaster preparedness for effective response and promoting the concept of “Build Back Better” in recovery, rehabilitation, and reconstruction. Together, these principles, targets, and priorities provide a comprehensive approach to reducing disaster risks and building resilient societies.
